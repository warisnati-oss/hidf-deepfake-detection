Can a computer catch deepfakes that people can't?

This page explains the project work so far for teammates who are new to machine learning (ML). We use HiDF, a 2025 dataset of face-swap photos that human reviewers could not tell apart from real photos. We built several detectors that look at a photo and answer "real" or "fake", then checked carefully that the results are trustworthy. Phases 1–5 are done. The slide deck (Phase 6) is next.

Key terms (read this first)

The words used on the rest of the page, in plain language.

Model / classifier
A program that learns a rule from examples instead of being told the rule. Ours takes a photo and outputs a score from 0 (real) to 1 (fake).
Label
The correct answer for each example ("real" or "fake"). The model sees labels only while training.
Train / validation / test split
We divide the data into three piles. Train (72 %): the model learns from it. Validation (14 %): we use it to make choices, like when to stop training. Test (14 %): touched once at the very end, like a final exam the model has never seen.
Threshold
The score above which we say "fake". We pick it on validation, never on test.
Accuracy
Share of test photos labelled correctly.
AUC
How well the scores rank fakes above reals, for every possible threshold. 1.0 = perfect, 0.5 = random guessing. Useful because it does not depend on one threshold.
False positive / false negative
False positive: a real photo called fake (a false alarm). False negative: a fake that slipped through. A confusion matrix is the 2 × 2 table that counts both.
Features (classical approach)
Numbers we design by hand from image-processing tools, such as texture, noise or frequency content. A simple model then learns which numbers matter.
Deep learning / neural network
A model with millions of adjustable numbers (weights) that learns its own features directly from pixels.
Pretrained backbone
A big network someone else already trained on millions of photos (here DINOv3 by Meta). We reuse its "visual knowledge" and adapt it to deepfakes.
Fine-tuning, LoRA
Fine-tuning = continuing training on our data. LoRA trains only small add-on pieces instead of the whole network, which is cheaper and harder to overfit.
Zero-shot
Using a model with no training on our data at all, just asking it.
Augmentation
Randomly blurring, compressing, cropping or flipping training photos so the model learns to cope with messy images.
Overfitting
When a model memorises the training examples instead of learning a general rule. It then does badly on new photos.
Leakage
When the model can get the answer through a back door (for example the same photo in train and test, or a file-format clue), so the score looks better than the model really is.
Confidence interval (CI)
A range that shows how much a score could change with a different test set. If two models' ranges overlap, we can't call one better.
Grad-CAM
A heat map that shows which part of the photo pushed the model toward its answer.
Epoch
One full pass of the model over the training data.
GPU
The graphics chip that makes training fast. One full training run took between about 3 and 12 hours here.

Phase 1

The data: how a HiDF fake is made

Every fake starts from two real photos. The base photo gives the scene, hair and body. The donor gives a face. A face-swap tool pastes the donor's inner face onto the base. Only about 11 % of the pixels change, so the background is identical to a real photo.

Size

69,831 images at 1024 × 1024 px: 38.6K real, 31.2K fake. After cleaning, 69,476 kept.

Cleaning

We removed 235 "real" files that were byte-for-byte copies of fakes and 50 fakes where the swap had failed, and fixed 458 wrong file names.

Why: a wrong label teaches the model the wrong thing.

Split by person

A fake, its base and its donor always land in the same pile. No person appears in both train and test.

Why: if the model saw a person in training, it could recognise the person instead of the swap. That would be cheating.

Phase 2

Preprocessing: getting images ready

Four steps, named P1–P4 in the documents.

P1

Same compression for all

Every image is saved the same way (JPEG, then resized to 512 px).

Real and fake files were saved differently. File size alone could separate them (AUC 0.99) without looking at any face. P1 removes that clue.

P2

Augmentation

Random crop, flip, colour change, blur, JPEG and noise, on training images only.

Makes the deep models cope with damaged photos.

P3

Find the face

MediaPipe draws the face outline. It covers 96 % of the swapped area.

Used for face crops and for the classical features.

P4

Hand-made features

247 numbers per image: frequency (FFT), noise, skin texture (LBP), colour, and the seam at the face edge.

Input to the classical model.

First leak found and fixed. Before P1, a model that saw only the background (face blanked out) could still tell real from fake. After P1 it scores 0.51, which is guessing. This is why we became careful about leakage early.

Phases 3 and 4

The models we compared

From the simplest (no training) to the most advanced. The course asks for both classical and deep-learning methods.

Zero-shot

Ask a big model, no training

CLIP compares the face with the text "a real photo" vs "a face-swapped photo". We also tried SigLIP 2, PE and Qwen2.5-VL (a chat model asked yes/no).

Shows how far you get without any training. Best: 90.6 % accuracy.

Classical

Hand-made features + gradient boosting

The 247 P4 numbers go into gradient boosting (many small decision trees voting).

The image-processing approach from the course. Found that swapped skin is smoother and less noisy than real skin.

M1

EfficientNet-B4

A classic image network. We train all 17.6 million weights. This is the model the HiDF paper used.

The standard baseline, so we can compare with the paper.

M2

GenD on DINOv3

Uses the big pretrained DINOv3 network almost frozen. Only 101 thousand weights are trained (0.03 %).

Tests whether a strong pretrained model needs almost no training.

M3

DINOv3 + LoRA

Same DINOv3, adapted with LoRA: 12.7 million trained weights (about 4 %). In the style of the NTIRE 2026 challenge winner.

The current state-of-the-art recipe.

12 runs

Each deep model in 4 settings

Baseline (full image, 224 px, augmentation on), then change one thing at a time: face crop only, augmentation off, higher resolution.

Changing one thing at a time shows what each choice really does.

For reference, the HiDF paper reports AUC 0.70 for detectors that were not trained on HiDF. Training on HiDF itself is what makes the difference.

How to read this

  • Zero-shot < classical < deep learning. These gaps are much bigger than the confidence intervals, so they are real.
  • The three deep models' intervals overlap. On clean photos we can't say one is better. All 12 deep runs land between 99.6 % and 99.8 %.
  • Almost every mistake is a missed fake, not a false alarm. That comes from the threshold chosen on validation.

What the 4 settings taught us

  • Face crop: about the same as the full image. The models already focus on the face.
  • Augmentation off: just as good on clean photos, but much worse on damaged ones (see next section).
  • Higher resolution: no clear gain, and far slower. M3 at high resolution took 13.5 h per epoch, so it ran only 2 epochs.

Robustness: what happens to damaged photos?

Photos on social media get compressed, resized and blurred. We damaged the test photos in 9 ways and tested again, without retraining.

Why this matters. The classical model relies on fine skin texture. Blur and noise erase that texture. The deep models were trained with augmentation (step P2), so they learned clues that survive damage: under all 9 damage types M1 stays ≥ 98.3 % accurate, M2 ≥ 96.6 %, M3 ≥ 98.6 %. This is the clearest reason to prefer deep learning here.

Where do the models look?

A high score is only convincing if the model looks at the swapped face, not at something unrelated.

Face blanked out

We greyed out the face and re-tested all 12 deep runs. AUC fell to 0.51–0.53 (guessing). So the models use the face, not the background.

Heat on the swap

Share of heat inside the true swap region: M1 47 %, M3 32 %, M2 24 %, which is 1.5–2.9× more than chance. The hottest point lands on the swap 82–91 % of the time.

Typical mistakes

All models miss the weakest swaps. The classical model also calls retouched real photos "fake", because smooth, edited skin looks like a swap to it.

Fairness: does it work equally for everyone?

We split the test results by race, gender and age of the person in the photo. The deep models make at most 1.9 % errors in every group. The classical model's gaps are larger (it misses 2–6 % of fakes depending on group).

One pattern holds for every model: photos of Asian people get the most false alarms. M2 and M3 also miss the most fakes on Asian faces, while the classical model and M1 miss the most on Black faces. Group size does not explain it: Indian faces are the smallest group (1.8 % of training) and are not the worst. We report this as a finding; we did not fix it.

Leakage checks: are the scores too good to be true?

99.8 % is suspicious, so we listed 16 possible leaks and checked each one. Below are the five extra tests run after Phase 5. The earlier checks (identity split, file-format fix, face blanking, thresholds set on validation only) are in LEAKAGE.md.

Conclusion. The near-perfect scores are real for this dataset. They are normal for a detector trained and tested on the same kind of fakes: other datasets show about 99 % in that setting too.

Limits and what's next

What we can't claim

  • Every HiDF fake comes from one face-swap tool. We don't know how the models do on deepfakes made by other tools. That would need a test on a different dataset.
  • DINOv3 was pretrained by Meta on a huge photo collection that might include some of the same real photos. We can't test that.
  • M3 at high resolution only trained for 2 epochs because it was so slow.
